How long it takes to build a nuclear reactor in America

Every power reactor the United States has started building since 1949 — finished, abandoned, or still going up today. Each line runs from the day concrete was first poured to the day the reactor entered commercial service, or to the day the project was halted. Height is the reactor's net electrical capacity; color is its technology, or the licence it was built under if you switch it. Hover any line for the company that built it, the reactor's history, and its licensing pathway.

Vertical position is net electrical capacity in megawatts. A handful of demonstration and research reactors currently being built produce no electricity for the grid, or have not published a net rating; those sit on the zero line. Reactors with identical capacity and identical dates — sister units at the same plant, poured together and finished together — are nudged a few pixels apart so you can tell them apart.

Two ways to lay out the time axis. One timeline puts all ninety years on a single axis. That is the honest view, but it has a problem: the construction boom of 1967 to 1974 accounts for well over half of every reactor ever started in America, and on a ninety‑year axis those eight years are a narrow, illegible band. By decade breaks the chart into one stacked panel per decade of construction start, so each era gets the full width of the page. Every panel uses the same number of years and the same number of pixels per megawatt, which means a line's length, slope and height mean the same thing in every panel and the decades can be read against one another. The panels are not all the same height, but only because each is cropped to the tallest reactor its own decade started — nothing in the 1950s was much above 800 MW, so a panel running to 1,400 would be half empty. Cropping the quiet decades hands that space to the crowded ones without distorting anything, because the scale itself never changes: the gap between the 400 and 800 MW gridlines is identical in every panel that has both. Each panel runs past its own ten years so that builds begun late in a decade still finish on screen; the faintly shaded region on the right of each panel is that spillover into later years. A few builds outran even that — Watts Bar 2 above all — and end in a double chevron meaning the line continues beyond the panel. Zoom and pan are switched off in this view, because every panel carries its own time axis and there is no longer a single window to move.

What counts as "never commercial." Reactors in that category were built and, in most cases, generated power, but were government demonstration or experimental units rather than utility products sold into a rate base — Shippingport, EBR‑I and EBR‑II, the Sodium Reactor Experiment, Hanford N, Elk River, Hallam and their peers. Shoreham is the exception that proves the category: it was finished, licensed to full power, then sold to New York State for one dollar and dismantled without ever selling a kilowatt‑hour commercially. Utility‑owned reactors that did operate commercially, however briefly, stay in the commercial group — including Three Mile Island 2, which ran for three months.

Where a completed reactor's line ends. Finishing a reactor is not a single event, so the line records two. The solid bar stops at first grid connection — the day the turbine was first synchronised to the electrical system and the plant put power onto the wires. The faded extension that follows runs to commercial operation, the later day the owner formally declared the unit in commercial service. For most plants the gap is a few months of power ascension testing. Occasionally it is much longer and tells you something: Fort St. Vrain took two and a half years, and Virgil C. Summer 1 took fourteen months. Reactors that never reached a grid have no tail, and neither do the cancelled projects.

Abandoned projects end on the date construction was formally halted or cancelled, which is not always the date work physically stopped; several sites sat in limbo for years first. Bellefonte, Zimmer and WNP‑1 were all more than half built. Zimmer was finished as a coal plant. Watts Bar 2, by contrast, appears as a completed reactor with a 43‑year line: it was mothballed in 1985 and restarted in 2007.

Built versus designed only. The five outcome toggles separate reactors that physically exist from those that do not. The first two categories are finished reactors that ran. "Part built, then abandoned" reactors have real concrete and, in several cases, an installed pressure vessel. "Being built now" means safety‑related nuclear construction has actually begun. The last category, drawn in faint dots with hollow markers, is design and licensing only — a permit, an application under review, or a federal agreement exists, but no reactor has been built. Several of these projects have held publicised groundbreakings; those events were site clearing, transmission work or gas generation, not reactor construction, so the dates shown are the developer's or the regulator's current targets rather than a record of work done. Among the currently licensed advanced‑reactor cohort, only Hermes 1, Hermes 2 and Kemmerer 1 have begun safety‑related construction.

The licensing pathway is a second, independent way to read the chart, and you can colour the lines by it. Almost every commercial reactor in America was licensed in two steps under 10 CFR Part 50: a construction permit first, then a separate operating licence years later once the plant was finished and inspected. Reactors that only ever got the permit — every cancelled project, plus the demonstration reactors going up right now — are a different bucket, and the split makes the abandonment wave of the late 1970s legible at a glance. Part 52, introduced in the 1990s, replaced the two steps with a single combined licence; Vogtle 3 and 4 are the only reactors ever completed under it. Federally owned reactors, from the early national‑laboratory experiments through today's Department of Energy pilot‑programme units at Idaho, were never NRC‑licensed at all and are authorised by DOE instead. Military reactors — the Army's compact plants, the Air Force's PM‑1, the Navy's land‑based prototypes — sit outside civilian licensing entirely. A handful of projects, Bodega Bay and Black Fox among them, never received any licence before being withdrawn. Note that there is no 10 CFR Part 57 for reactors; the NRC's new technology‑inclusive framework is Part 53, and no reactor has yet been licensed under it, so it does not appear here.

Dates in the tooltip marked approximate are either estimated from a start year with no published day, or are forward projections. Reactors under construction today end at their operator's projected completion date, drawn as a dotted extension past the present.

Ownership reflects the most recent public transfer. Several shut‑down plants are now held by decommissioning specialists rather than the original utility, and a few cancelled sites have passed to industrial developers, port authorities or state parks. Photographs on the hover card are pulled live from Wikipedia and Wikimedia Commons where one exists for that plant; many demonstration and cancelled reactors have no public photograph, and nothing is shown for them.

Reactors that have achieved criticality are flagged with a red ring on the chart and a red band on the hover card. Criticality is the moment a reactor first sustains a nuclear chain reaction — the point at which it becomes a working reactor rather than a building. It is the milestone the current cohort of demonstration reactors is racing toward, and five of them have reached it since June 2026: Antares’ Mark‑0, Valar Atomics’ Ward 250, Deployable Energy’s Unity, Aalo Atomics’ Aalo‑X and Oklo’s Groves Isotope Test Reactor. All five are federally authorised units on or beside Department of Energy land, and all five are very small — between zero and ten megawatts electrical — which is why they sit almost on the zero line on a chart scaled for gigawatt‑class plants. Switch the capacity axis to Compressed to pull them clear of the baseline, or press Achieved criticality to isolate them.
