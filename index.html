How long it takes to build a nuclear reactor in America

Every power reactor the United States has started building since 1949 — finished, abandoned, or still going up today. Each line runs from the day concrete was first poured to the day the reactor entered commercial service, or to the day the project was halted. Height is the reactor's net electrical capacity; color is its technology, or the licence it was built under if you switch it. Vertical position is net electrical capacity in megawatts. A handful of demonstration and research reactors currently being built produce no electricity for the grid, or have not published a net rating; those sit on the zero line. Reactors with identical capacity and identical dates — sister units at the same plant, poured together and finished together — are nudged a few pixels apart so you can tell them apart.

What counts as "never commercial." Reactors in that category were built and, in most cases, generated power, but were government demonstration or experimental units rather than utility products sold into a rate base — Shippingport, EBR‑I and EBR‑II, the Sodium Reactor Experiment, Hanford N, Elk River, Hallam and their peers. Shoreham is the exception that proves the category: it was finished, licensed to full power, then sold to New York State for one dollar and dismantled without ever selling a kilowatt‑hour commercially. Utility‑owned reactors that did operate commercially, however briefly, stay in the commercial group — including Three Mile Island 2, which ran for three months.

Abandoned projects end on the date construction was formally halted or cancelled, which is not always the date work physically stopped; several sites sat in limbo for years first. Bellefonte, Zimmer and WNP‑1 were all more than half built. Zimmer was finished as a coal plant. Watts Bar 2, by contrast, appears as a completed reactor with a 43‑year line: it was mothballed in 1985 and restarted in 2007.

Built versus designed only. The five outcome toggles separate reactors that physically exist from those that do not. The first two categories are finished reactors that ran. "Part built, then abandoned" reactors have real concrete and, in several cases, an installed pressure vessel. "Being built now" means safety‑related nuclear construction has actually begun. The last category, drawn in faint dots with hollow markers, is design and licensing only — a permit, an application under review, or a federal agreement exists, but no reactor has been built. Several of these projects have held publicised groundbreakings; those events were site clearing, transmission work or gas generation, not reactor construction, so the dates shown are the developer's or the regulator's current targets rather than a record of work done. Among the currently licensed advanced‑reactor cohort, only Hermes 1, Hermes 2 and Kemmerer 1 have begun safety‑related construction.

The licensing pathway is a second, independent way to read the chart, and you can colour the lines by it. Almost every commercial reactor in America was licensed in two steps under 10 CFR Part 50: a construction permit first, then a separate operating licence years later once the plant was finished and inspected. Reactors that only ever got the permit — every cancelled project, plus the demonstration reactors going up right now — are a different bucket, and the split makes the abandonment wave of the late 1970s legible at a glance. Part 52, introduced in the 1990s, replaced the two steps with a single combined licence; Vogtle 3 and 4 are the only reactors ever completed under it. Federally owned reactors, from the early national‑laboratory experiments through today's Department of Energy pilot‑programme units at Idaho, were never NRC‑licensed at all and are authorised by DOE instead. Military reactors — the Army's compact plants, the Air Force's PM‑1, the Navy's land‑based prototypes — sit outside civilian licensing entirely. A handful of projects, Bodega Bay and Black Fox among them, never received any licence before being withdrawn. Note that there is no 10 CFR Part 57 for reactors; the NRC's new technology‑inclusive framework is Part 53, and no reactor has yet been licensed under it, so it does not appear here.

Dates in the tooltip marked approximate are either estimated from a start year with no published day, or are forward projections. Reactors under construction today end at their operator's projected completion date, drawn as a dotted extension past the present.

The energy figure on hover is an estimate, not metered output: it multiplies net capacity by time online by a capacity factor typical of the reactor's operating era — about 60% for units that ran mainly in the 1970s and 80s, about 88% for units running in the 2000s and later. Treat it as an order of magnitude for comparing reactors, not as a reported total. Ownership reflects the most recent public transfer; several shut‑down plants are now held by decommissioning specialists rather than the original utility, and a few cancelled sites have passed to industrial developers, port authorities or state parks.

Data compiled from public records of U.S. commercial power reactors, cancelled reactor projects, and current construction permits. Capacities are net electrical output in megawatts as designed or as last uprated.
